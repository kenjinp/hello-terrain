## Overview

The same Earth-sized cube-sphere as the [Earth-Scale Planet](/examples/earth-planet) example (authalic radius **6,371,007.2 m**), built with `invert: true` so the continents grow *inward*. A small sun sits at the centre of the world, five bodies orbit it, and you stand on the inside of the shell with the far side of the planet for a sky.

```tsx
const topology = createCubeSphereTopology({ radius: SHELL_RADIUS, invert: true });
```

That one flag is the only change to the terrain setup from the outside-in planet, apart from a different elevation function. Everything else on this page — the light, the shadows, the air, the water and the cameras — is example code layered on the terrain's `positionNode` and runtime queries.

### Controls

- **Tour** cuts between the six scripted shots every 18 seconds. Click a shot to hold it.
- **Walk** drops you onto the nearest dry land. Click the canvas to capture the mouse, **WASD** to move, **Space** to jump, **Shift** to sprint, **Esc** to release.
- **Fly** is the altitude-scaled fly camera from the Earth-Scale Planet example.
- **[** and **]** step the clock back and forward an hour (hold **Shift** for six), so you can dial in dusk or an eclipse without waiting. The example needs focus for hotkeys — click it first.

## How it works

### Night comes from a moon, not from rotation

With the sun at the centre, every point on the shell has it at the zenith, permanently. Nothing rotates into shadow, so the day/night cycle has to be cast.

**Nyx** does that. It is a dark body 820 km in radius orbiting only 1,000 km from the sun, which lets it block a cone of light about 55° wide. One orbit is one day; wherever its shadow falls, it is night. Because the sun is a disc and not a point, the edge of the shadow is a penumbra roughly 2,500 km across — dusk and dawn. Nyx's orbit is tilted 23.4° and precesses, so the band of night wanders over the year and the poles never see full night.

The four outer moons are too small and too far from the sun for that. They cast travelling eclipse spots a few hundred kilometres wide instead.

None of this uses a shadow map. The only occluders are spheres, and all light comes from one point, so "how much of the sun can this point see?" has a closed form: for each body, compare its disc with the cross-section of the cone that joins the point to the sun.

```ts
const along = dot(body.xyz.sub(p), toSun);             // where the sun ray passes the body
const miss = length(body.xyz.sub(p).sub(toSun.mul(along))); // …and by how much
const coneRadius = SUN_RADIUS * along / sunDistance;   // size of the sun's disc there
const overlap = 1 - smoothstep(abs(bodyRadius - coneRadius), bodyRadius + coneRadius, miss);
```

The same function shades the terrain, the water, the moons (they eclipse each other) and every sample of the atmosphere.

### An atmosphere that gets thicker towards the walls

The air follows the model from Maxime Heckel's [On Rendering the Sky, Sunsets, and Planets](https://blog.maximeheckel.com/posts/on-rendering-the-sky-sunsets-and-planets/): a raymarch that accumulates Rayleigh scattering, Mie scattering and ozone absorption along each view ray. The inversion is in the density function — altitude is measured from the shell towards the centre, so the air is densest against the ground and the middle of the world is vacuum:

```ts
const altitude = SHELL_RADIUS - length(samplePosition);
const rayleigh = exp(-altitude / 8500);
```

Three things differ from a conventional sky shader:

- **It still runs as a post-processing pass**, even though there is no sky to fill in — every ray ends on the shell, a moon or the sun. See [Performance](#performance) for why.
- **The light march is free.** A normal planet needs a second loop from every sample towards the sun. Here sunlight always travels radially and density depends only on radius, so the optical depth between the sun and a sample is `H · exp(-altitude / H)` — one `exp`, no loop.
- **Most of each ray is vacuum.** A ray can cross 12,700 km with air only in the first and last hundred. Each ray is clipped against the top of the atmosphere and marched as two short segments, with samples bunched towards the ground.

The shadow test runs per sample, so Nyx's shadow darkens the air as well as the land, and the moons drag visible shafts through the haze.

Since the sun never sits low in the sky, there is no long slanted light path to redden it. The warm terminator comes from limb darkening instead: as Nyx covers the disc, only the sun's dimmer, redder rim is left (`terminatorWarmth`).

### Terrain with things to measure against

A featureless shell gives the eye nothing to judge 6,371 km by, so the world is laid out by a small model of plate tectonics (`tectonics()` in `elevation.ts`). The surface is cut into about thirty Voronoi plates, each continental or oceanic and each with a drift direction, which gives every border a type:

| Border | What it builds |
| --- | --- |
| Continent colliding with continent | A broad, high range |
| Ocean plate under a continent | A coastal cordillera, with a trench offshore |
| Ocean colliding with ocean | A trench and a volcanic island arc behind it |
| Ocean plates separating | A mid-ocean ridge |
| A continent pulling apart | A rift valley, flooding into long lakes |

Fractal noise then erodes and extends the plate outlines into coasts. On top of that: worn-down ancient ranges away from any border, plateaus with abrupt edges, patches of hill country, valleys that flood where they cut below sea level, hotspot tracks of seamounts whose tallest peaks surface as island chains, and a continental shelf — a terrace about 130 m down that ends at a shelf break and drops to a 4 km abyssal plain. The water shader brightens shelf seas, which is what makes them visible from across the world.

Ranges are built from ridged noise squeezed across the plate border, so crests run parallel to it the way fold belts do.

Three details make that hold up from every distance:

- **Octaves are faded per tile.** The elevation callback receives `tileLevel`, which gives the tile's vertex spacing. Any octave finer than a couple of vertices is replaced by its mean. Without this, a far-side tile with vertices 50 km apart samples a 7 km ridge at random and produces shards.
- **The material puts the missing octaves back.** A far-side range would otherwise arrive as a smooth swell. The terrain material re-evaluates the ridge function per pixel at two band limits — what the tile carries and what the pixel can show — and uses the difference: the extra height moves the snow and tree lines, and its analytic gradient bends the normal. Tile size comes from screen-space derivatives of the tile UVs, so no per-tile data is needed. Close up, where the mesh already carries every octave, the whole branch is skipped.
- **The noise lattice is wrapped.** Hashing raw lattice coordinates in the tens of thousands (a kilometre-scale octave on an Earth-sized sphere) runs `sin` out of float32 precision. Wrapping the lattice every 256 cells keeps hash inputs small.

Tree line and snow line sit lower where nights are long, and snow only holds on the gentler slopes, which is what draws the rock faces.

### Clouds and their shadows

A cloud deck sits between 1.5 and 6.5 km above the sea, so the tallest ranges stand through it. It is marched in the same full-screen pass as the air, and follows the structure of the volumetric clouds in [three-geospatial](https://github.com/takram-design-engineering/three-geospatial): a low-frequency weather pattern decides where cloud systems are and how tall they grow, a Perlin–Worley volume gives the base shapes, and finer Worley noise erodes the edges. The noise is two small tileable 3D textures generated on the CPU at start-up: a 64³ shape volume and a 48³ weather volume, so a march sample costs one texture tap under clear sky and five inside a cloud.

That library targets an ordinary planet under a distant sun, and shadows its clouds with cascaded Beer shadow maps. Here the geometry allows something simpler. The density is kept *separable* — weather that depends only on direction from the centre, times a profile that depends only on altitude — and all sunlight travels along radii. So the optical depth of cloud between any point and the sun has a closed form: the column's total depth times the fraction of the profile above the point. One function, two texture taps, no shadow maps:

- on the terrain and the sea it is the cloud shadow;
- inside the cloud it is the self-shadowing that darkens the bases;
- at every sample of the atmosphere march it is the volumetric shadow — the shafts of light under broken cloud.

Each pixel's samples are offset by a different fraction of a step, which trades banding for fine grain. The clouds also act as a veil inside the air: only the air in front of them hazes them, and air behind them only shows through by their transmittance.

### Water

The sea is the terrain drawn a second time. A wrapper around the terrain's `positionNode` pulls every vertex onto the sea-level sphere and keeps the distance it moved as the water depth:

```ts
const displaced = vec3(terrainPositionNode).toVar();
const radius = length(displaced);
seabedDepth.assign(radius.sub(SHELL_RADIUS));
return displaced.div(radius).mul(SHELL_RADIUS);
```

Passing one `useTerrain` handle to two `<Terrain>` components shares the quadtree and the compute pass, so the water costs a draw call and gets tessellated exactly where the camera needs it. The depth test against the terrain draws the coastline.

In the fragment shader, depth drives Beer–Lambert absorption (red dies in a few metres, which is what turns shallows turquoise), Fresnel blends that with a reflection of the sky — a short atmosphere march towards the far side of the world — and a sum of travelling waves perturbs the normal. Waves shorter than a few pixels are faded out of the normal and into the width of the sun's glint.

### Precision at 6,371 km

A float32 world position at this radius is quantised to about half a metre, which is far too coarse for metre-long waves or ground detail. Both are evaluated in camera-relative coordinates instead: the fragment's offset from the camera is exact, and the camera's own position is supplied modulo 8,192 m from float64 on the CPU. Every wave vector and noise lattice is snapped to that period, so the pattern is seamless when the origin wraps.

The terrain mesh itself still has the precision floor described in the Earth-Scale Planet example. That is why the walker defaults to a 12 m eye height and a brisk 60 m/s stride — lower either in the `player` controls to see the vertices swim.

### Standing on the walls

`WalkerCamera` keeps a position and a heading tangent to the ground. "Up" is the direction to the sun, so it changes as you move; each frame the heading is flattened against the new up (parallel transport), which keeps the view steady the whole way round the world. The ground height comes from the terrain's runtime query:

```ts
const sample = terrain.runtime.surfaceQuery.sampleTerrainByPosition(position);
const ground = Math.min(sample.position.length(), SHELL_RADIUS); // land, or the sea surface
position.setLength(ground - heightAboveGround - eyeHeight);
```

Gravity is a scalar acting on `heightAboveGround`, pushing outward. The cinematic shots use the same query to stay clear of mountains.

## Performance

The first version of this example marched the atmosphere inside every material. It ran poorly at ground level and noticeably better looking straight up, for two reasons that compound:

- **A logarithmic depth buffer disables early depth rejection.** It writes depth from the fragment shader, so the GPU cannot discard a hidden fragment before shading it. Every layer runs its full shader: the seabed under the sea, the water sphere under every continent, and the stacked skirt walls around the camera.
- **Near the horizon, terrain triangles are slivers.** Tiles are sized by distance, not by the angle they are seen at, so ground seen edge-on is covered in triangles a fraction of a pixel tall. Each one still shades a full 2×2 pixel block.

Looking up, the far side of the world is seen face-on and neither applies, hence the difference.

The fix is to make the expensive part independent of geometry:

- The atmosphere is a full-screen pass (`atmospherePass.ts`) that reads depth and marches once per pixel, however many triangles or layers are behind that pixel.
- That march, clouds included, runs in a reduced-resolution target — a quarter of the pixels at the default 0.5 — because what it produces is soft. The composite upsamples it with depth-aware weights, so haze does not smear across silhouettes. Only the air's transmittance, which needs no lighting or texture lookups, is marched at full resolution.
- Each reduced-resolution ray is cast through the centre of one specific scene pixel and uses that pixel's depth. Casting it through the boundary between pixels, where a half-resolution texel naturally sits, pairs a ray with a neighbour's depth and shows up as bands.
- Water fragments under land are discarded before any work, and sea floor too deep to see skips its shading.
- The scene is rendered without multisampling; FXAA runs after tone mapping instead.
